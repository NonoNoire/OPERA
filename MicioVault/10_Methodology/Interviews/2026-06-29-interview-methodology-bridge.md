# Interview methodology bridge

## Function of this note

This note translates the archived methodological deep research into an operational starting point for the doctoral interview work. The source report should remain an archived methodological source. This note is the working bridge: it decides what to do next, what to avoid, and how to convert the material into interview design.

Source: `MicioVault/10_Methodology/Interviews/2026-06-29-deep-research-methodology-interviews.md`

## Core methodological decision

Do **not** start by writing a definitive interview script or a definitive codebook.

Start by building a flexible methodological apparatus composed of:

- research questions;
- epistemic unit of the interview;
- purposive sample map;
- sensitizing concepts;
- artifact elicitation prompts;
- pilot interview guide;
- reflexive memo system;
- audit trail;
- provisional analysis strategy.

The strongest current direction is:

> semi-structured interview + artifact elicitation/document analysis + reflexive or abductive thematic analysis, with possible grounded-theory sensitivity for processual categories.

This fits the doctoral project because the object is not a stable variable to measure, but a processual field: memory, oblivion, documentation, creative traces, tacit knowledge, archive, loss, material/digital persistence, and transformation of practice.

## Immediate course of action

### 1. Define the epistemic unit

The interview should not ask for generic opinions about memory, archive, or artistic research. It should make specific research objects narratable.

Possible epistemic units:

- a creative process;
- a moment of loss, deletion, forgetting, deterioration, or transformation;
- an artifact that preserves processual knowledge;
- a decision that changed the direction of a work;
- a document, archive, sketch, file, prototype, note, or trace;
- a practice of documenting or refusing documentation;
- a tension between what is preserved and what is necessarily lost.

Working formulation:

> The interview investigates how creative, artistic, design, or organizational actors produce, preserve, transform, lose, narrate, or externalize processual knowledge through artifacts, traces, documents, archives, and situated practices.

### 2. Build a purposive sample map

The first sample should be intentional, not snowball-only. Snowballing can come later to reach hidden or specialist nodes.

Provisional participant families:

- artists working with process, memory, archive, deterioration, documentation, digital traces, or embodied knowledge;
- curators, archivists, and cultural mediators dealing with preservation, exhibition, documentation, and institutional memory;
- academics and researchers in artistic research, design research, digital humanities, STS, qualitative methods, or memory studies;
- institutional figures connected to the Accademia and to the doctoral research environment;
- GRADED or other organizational/innovation actors, but only where the connection to documentation, process knowledge, or transformation of practice is methodologically defensible.

Critical constraint:

> Do not force all participants into the same role. The same guide should have a shared backbone and role-specific modules.

### 3. Create a sensitizing matrix, not a final codebook

A final codebook before fieldwork would be methodologically premature. A sensitizing matrix is appropriate.

Provisional sensitizing concepts:

- processual memory;
- tacit knowledge;
- artifact as trace;
- documentation as transformation;
- archive as selection;
- oblivion as loss, filtering, or productive erasure;
- material degradation;
- digital persistence;
- metadata and contextual loss;
- institutional memory;
- authorship and control;
- preservation versus fossilization;
- embodied practice;
- translation between practice and document;
- gap between process and final output.

Each concept should later become a memo, not immediately a code.

### 4. Draft a pilot interview guide by blocks

The guide should be modular and semi-structured.

Recommended blocks:

1. Role, trajectory, and context.
2. Description of a concrete project, work, process, or case.
3. Processual decisions and moments of transformation.
4. Traces, artifacts, documents, sketches, files, prototypes, archives.
5. Loss, forgetting, deterioration, deletion, gaps, omissions.
6. Tacit knowledge and what resists documentation.
7. Institutional, technical, or organizational constraints.
8. Reflection on memory, archive, and future access.
9. Closing: what the participant thinks the researcher should have asked but did not.

Do not write the questions in a way that already contains the expected analytical category.

Bad direction:

> In what way does your practice demonstrate the productive role of oblivion?

Better direction:

> Can you describe a moment when something from the process was lost, discarded, overwritten, or became inaccessible? What changed after that?

### 5. Add artifact elicitation

This should be central, not decorative.

Possible participant instruction:

> If possible, bring or show one material or digital trace connected to a creative/research/design process: a sketch, draft, file, photo, note, prototype, archive folder, version history, failed attempt, deleted fragment, or object that helps you remember how the work changed.

Possible probes:

- What does this artifact preserve?
- What does it fail to preserve?
- What would be unintelligible to someone who only saw this object/document/file?
- What part of the process lives elsewhere: body, habit, memory, software, context, relationship, institution?
- Has the meaning of this trace changed over time?

### 6. Pilot before formalizing

Pilot 1–2 interviews before treating the guide as stable.

After each pilot, write a memo on:

- questions that produced abstract answers;
- questions that produced rich episodes;
- leading or over-theorized formulations;
- concepts introduced by the participant that were not anticipated;
- points where artifact elicitation worked or failed;
- changes to the participant map.

### 7. Choose the first analysis strategy

Default analytical strategy:

> reflexive thematic analysis with abductive movement between data, theory, and artistic practice.

Use grounded-theory techniques selectively for processual categories, but do not claim full grounded theory unless the project also adopts theoretical sampling, constant comparison, and systematic theory generation.

Do not treat intercoder reliability as the main criterion of rigor if the analysis remains interpretative and reflexive. Peer debriefing, audit trail, thick description, reflexive memos, and negative cases are more coherent.

## Critical risks

### Risk 1: over-theorized interviews

Because the project already has strong theoretical language, the danger is asking questions that only confirm the existing vocabulary. The interview should first obtain episodes, artifacts, decisions, contradictions, and situations. Theory should enter later, through analysis.

### Risk 2: confusing topic blocks with themes

Interview blocks are not analytical themes. A block called “archive” does not mean that “archive” is a finding. Themes must be constructed from patterns of meaning in the data.

### Risk 3: stretching GRADED too far

GRADED can be relevant if framed through documentation, process knowledge, innovation practice, organizational memory, and translation of tacit knowledge into protocols or artifacts. It becomes weak if forced directly into the same frame as artistic memory without a mediating methodological argument.

### Risk 4: treating AI-generated research as bibliography

The archived deep research is useful as a scaffold, but its citation markers are session-bound and must be reconstructed. Before thesis use, each bibliographic reference must be verified independently and added to Zotero.

Create `sensitizing-matrix-v0.md` before the final interview guide.

Minimum structure:

| Area | Sensitizing concept | Why it matters | Possible interview episode | Possible artifact | Bias risk |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Memory/documentation | Processual memory | Captures how practice survives beyond final output | A project whose process changed over time | Sketch, file history, archive folder | Asking too abstractly |
| Oblivion/loss | Productive or accidental loss | Distinguishes erasure, deterioration, forgetting, omission | A lost draft or inaccessible file | Missing file, corrupted archive, discarded prototype | Romanticizing loss |
| Tacit knowledge | What resists documentation | Connects artistic practice to knowledge production | A gesture or decision hard to explain | Tool, material sample, body technique | Forcing verbal explanation |
| Archive/institution | Selection and exclusion | Connects individual practice to systems of preservation | What gets archived and what is left out | Archive structure, catalogue, metadata | Treating archive as neutral |
| Digital/material persistence | Twin, trace, version, residue | Links directly to Oblivion’s material/digital problem | A physical/digital transformation | Scan, digital twin, degraded object | Assuming digital equals preservation |
